★★★★☆ Patient, exact, and it earns its withholding
✔ Verified PurchaseReviewed in the United Kingdom on 12 March

This is not a book that hurries. It opens with an inventory — a house in Broughty Ferry, six hundred and forty shares, “a charitable trust, the Cargill Bequest, whose purpose was the relief of deserving persons of the City of Dundee” — and the whole novel keeps that temperature.

Two strands: a Dundee estate room in 1976, and interludes in Amsterdam, Paris, Genoa and Port Tobacco where documents do the work that other novels give to dialogue. A receipt for one hundred and twelve barrels of salt beef tells you more about the Company of Scotland than a chapter of exposition would.

What makes it four rather than five: a few interludes read like briefings rather than scenes, and the seventeenth-century chapters are over almost as soon as they begin. The modern strand is the heart, and it is superb. Bring on Volume II.

★☆☆☆☆ An audit, not a novel
✔ Verified PurchaseReviewed in the United Kingdom on 9 March

I bought this expecting a Scottish family saga and got eleven documents in eighty years, plus a man counting barrels. Page after page is ledgers: “the warehouse book was ruled in two columns, and the columns were headed in and out.” Interesting once. By the fourth era I could not tell you a single thing about Maggie except that she has files on a long table and puts them there by half past nine.

The historical chapters are lectures with dates on top — Genoa 1557, Paris 1688 — and then they stop, sixty pages short of anything happening. Nobody in this book says what they want. It ends by promising Volume II, which annoyed me more than the ending itself.

Beautiful sentences, genuinely. But I read novels for people, and this one has decided people are paperwork. Two hundred pages of my life I'd like graded back.
